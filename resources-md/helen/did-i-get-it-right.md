Workplace Edge – Did I Get It Right?

# **1. Lesson Overview**

Lesson Title: Did I Get It Right?

Pathway (I, We, Us): I

Skill Focus: Active Clarification

Workplace Challenge: You nod in a meeting, smile on a video call, or reply “got it” in a message but later realize you're unsure what was actually agreed to. In fast paced or high stakes environments, we may hesitate to ask for clarity, fearing it signals incompetence. However, making assumptions can lead to costly errors, misalignment, or rework. This lesson helps learners build confidence using active clarification strategies, checking understanding in the moment, to build trust, ensure accuracy, and create space for others to clarify as well.

# **2. Learning Objectives**

Learners will be able to:

- Use paraphrasing language to confirm understanding in real-time.
- Demonstrate how to clarify instructions or agreements respectfully and clearly.
- Reflect on the impact of clarifying vs. assuming in workplace communication

# **3. Spark (5–10 minutes)**

** Short Story:** The Cost of Silence: When Arjun joined a new cross-functional team, he wanted to make a good first impression. In one kickoff meeting, the team lead said, “Make sure the quarterly summary has everything from the leadership brief.” Arjun nodded but wasn’t sure what “leadership brief” meant, it was never explained to him, and the team didn’t pause.

Instead of clarifying, Arjun tried to piece it together from old emails. He submitted the report on time, but it lacked key data that was expected. The project hit a stall, the team lost credibility with stakeholders, and Arjun felt humiliated.

His team wasn’t angry, but their trust in his attention to detail wavered. Arjun later shared, “I didn’t feel brave enough to ask. I thought I’d look incompetent.” Now, he says, “Asking that one question would’ve changed everything.”

Why this matters: This kind of moment isn’t rare. Quick agreement can be a coping strategy, or a cultural default, but when no one checks for alignment, teams lose both time and trust. As [moniquehelstrom.com](https://www.moniquehelstrom.com/bloginventory/understanding-miscommunication) notes, miscommunication frequently stems not from poor intent, but from a lack of shared clarity and emotionally safe space to slow down. When we assume understanding, we may unintentionally create distance, confusion, or frustration, especially across roles or communication styles.

*Add-on for Designers: Consider pairing this story with a textbox or reflection prompt like:*

- *“Have you ever stayed silent, then regretted it?”*
- *“What invisible strain might unspoken confusion be placing on your team?”*
- *“How does it feel when you’re not sure if someone understood you  and they don’t ask?”*

**Reflection Prompt:** Think about a recent moment at work when you nodded along in a meeting or said “okay” to a request, only later realizing you weren’t entirely clear what was being asked. What happened next?

Here’s how one team member put it: *“I didn’t want to look confused in front of the client, so I just nodded. Later I realized I hadn’t understood what they meant by ‘phase two’. My manager had to smooth it over, and I felt like I’d let the team down”.*

**One example: **During a fast moving product meeting, a designer nodded at a change request but wasn’t sure which version was approved. They didn’t clarify in the moment, assuming they could follow up later. The team launched the update, only to discover the wrong asset had been used. A two minute clarification could have saved hours of rework.

Here’s** another real-world example:** When Amal joined a new marketing team, she often replied “got it” in meetings, not wanting to slow things down. However, after a misinterpreted deadline caused a missed client handover, she realized she hadn’t understood the handoff process. Clarifying earlier could have protected both the timeline and the relationship.

You’re not alone. Many professionals, especially in fast-paced or multicultural teams, default to appearing agreeable even when details are fuzzy. The cost? confusion, duplicated work, rework, or even reputational damage.

This kind of passive misalignment isn’t rare, in fact, one of the most overlooked productivity drains is poor knowledge flow and unspoken misunderstanding (McKinsey, 2012). Silence is often interpreted as consent. Even in collaborative teams, “nods” and “got it” often reflect social ease more than true clarity. Especially in multicultural settings, quick affirmation may be polite, not literal.

However, it doesn’t have to be that way. In fact, asking a clarifying question, or paraphrasing what you heard, can build trust, signal professionalism, and model psychological safety for others.

Clarifying isn’t just about accuracy, it’s an act of inclusion. It shows others it’s safe to pause, ask, and speak up. When you clarify, you model the kind of communication that builds stronger teams and reduces fear of mistakes. You make it safer for others to do the same. Your tone and timing can help create a culture where people feel seen, heard, and encouraged to ask their own questions, especially those who may hesitate due to personality, power dynamics, or cultural background.

**Current State/Behaviour:** Passive agreement without full clarity.
**Desired State/Behaviour:** Using active clarification to confirm shared understanding.

# **4. Shift (20–25 minutes)**

Input: Text

Transcript/Link/File: In any workplace, but especially in diverse or fast-moving environments, assumptions are risky. Clarifying understanding helps avoid small misunderstandings that grow into larger consequences.

Paraphrasing is a simple but powerful strategy: you briefly restate what you heard in your own words and ask for confirmation. It shows you’re listening and helps uncover assumptions.

This is different from repeating back word for word. Repeating might capture the exact language, but paraphrasing checks that you understood the meaning. Repeating can sound robotic or overly formal, while paraphrasing is more natural and responsive.

Not every situation calls for the same tone. **Examples of Misunderstanding (What Not to Do):**

- “Sure, got it.” (Even when unclear). Note: “Got it” is a common informal phrase. But sometimes, people say it to sound agreeable, even if they’re unclear. It’s helpful to follow up with a clarifying question to avoid assumptions.
- Silence or nodding with confusion
- Repeating back word-for-word without processing

Language for Impact Focus: Teach learners to use phrases that:

- Invite confirmation without fear
- Show collaboration and respect
- Normalize clarification as a strength

Example in Action: What Clarifying Looks Like From a Team Lead

**Real-World Scenario: Clarifying as a Leadership Practice**

Clarifying doesn’t undermine your credibility, it models leadership. Here’s how one senior team lead described it in a team call: "Just to be sure I’ve got this right, when you say ‘client-ready,’ do you mean sharing with stakeholders or a dry run internally first? I don’t want to assume.”

No one questioned her capability. In fact, it showed her attention to detail, her respect for others’ interpretations, and her ability to lead by example. Her question gave permission for others to slow down too.

In many teams, especially fast paced or culturally diverse ones, leaders who clarify out loud are seen as thoughtful and reliable. They make it safe for everyone to ask questions, not just the most confident voices.

Tip: The higher your seniority or fluency, the more power you have to normalize clarification for others. Use that power to build trust.

**“Before & After” – Clarification in Action: **Compare these two versions of the same workplace moment. Notice what’s different.

Version 1: No Clarification (Misaligned) Manager: “We’ll need the deck finalized by midweek.”
Team member: “Got it.”
(But they submit it Thursday afternoon. Turns out “midweek” meant Wednesday EOD. The deck misses review.)

Reflection: What assumption was made here? How could this have affected trust, timing, or workflow?

Version 2: With Clarification Manager: “We’ll need the deck finalized by midweek.”
Team member: “Let me confirm,  does midweek mean end of day Wednesday, or sometime Thursday?”
Manager: “Wednesday EOD ideally, we want it reviewed Thursday.”
Team member: “Great, I’ll aim for Wednesday at 3 so you have extra buffer.”

Outcome: Roles and expectations are clear. The team stays aligned, and stress is reduced on both sides.

Useful Phrases:

- Let me check I’ve got this right, does that mean [X]?”
- “So, just to clarify, are we saying [Y]?”
- “Can I play that back to you in my own words?”
- “I want to make sure I’m on the same page, can I check something?”
- “Could you help me confirm I’ve understood this correctly?”
- “Would you mind if I repeated that in my own words, to double-check I’m aligned?”
- “Just so I don’t miss anything, do you mean [X] or [Y]?”
- “Let me make sure I’ve got it—are we talking about [example]?”

Tone Variations: Depending on your communication style or context, you might choose a more formal, indirect, or casual version. Try these:

- Formal: “Would you mind if I checked my understanding to ensure I’m aligned?”
- Indirect: “I may have missed a detail—could we revisit what you meant by [X]?”
- Casual: “Quick check—by ‘wrap up,’ do you mean ship or just finish dev?”

*Tone Tip:* Some cultures prefer indirect or softened requests, especially with senior colleagues. If direct questioning feels uncomfortable, try neutral phrases like:

- “I may have misunderstood, could I check something?”
- “Would you be open to me confirming that to avoid confusion?”

In high context cultures, clarifying too directly may feel confrontational. Using shared responsibility language like “Shall we clarify…” or “Would it help if we...” can signal care and inclusion.

**Language for Impact 2.0: Adapting by Context & Audience**

Clarifying successfully isn’t just about choosing a helpful phrase, it’s about choosing the right tone for the relationship, setting, and communication norms.

Below are sample phrases grouped by communication context. Try reading each one aloud. Which feel natural? Which feel too formal, too direct, or too vague for your workplace?

With a Peer or Close Team Member:

- “Quick check, by ‘done,’ do you mean dev complete or client-ready?”
- “Let me play that back to make sure I got it.”

Impact: These are casual and collaborative. Use them when relationships are established or hierarchy is low.

With a Senior or External Stakeholder

- “Just to confirm I’ve understood correctly, would that include the Q3 data or only Q2?”
- “Before I move forward, I want to check my understanding to make sure this aligns with your priorities.”

Impact: These soften tone and signal respect, especially in high-stakes settings.

In Multicultural or High-Context Teams

- “I may have misunderstood, would you be open to clarifying what success looks like here?”
- “Would it help if I checked what I heard before I take action?”

Impact: These use inclusive framing and invite shared clarity, which can reduce risk in indirect or hierarchical cultures.

Prompt: Before clarifying, ask yourself: ✔ What’s the formality level of this setting? ✔ What’s the power dynamic?
✔ How direct/indirect is my organization’s culture?

A slight tone shift can make your message feel collaborative vs. confrontational. For example, instead of “Do you mean this or that?”, try:  → “Can I check that detail, just so I don’t assume?”

Emotional Intelligence in Clarifying Moments: When you pause to clarify, you're not just improving accuracy, you're practicing emotional intelligence (EQ). Asking for clarity draws on:

- Self-awareness: Recognising when you feel uncertain, rushed, or hesitant to speak
- Empathy: Considering how unclear language or silence might impact others’ understanding or confidence
- Reappraisal: Reframing a moment of confusion not as a failure, but as an opportunity to co-create understanding
- Emotional regulation: Managing your fear of appearing uninformed, especially in high-stakes or hierarchical settings

For example: “I felt hesitant to speak up in that global planning call” → self-awareness
“I clarified quietly in chat to help others who might also be unsure” → empathy + psychological safety
“I reframed my question as a way to align before moving forward” → reappraisal

*Inclusive Note:* Some cultures rely more on context, nonverbal cues, or hierarchy when clarifying. Speaking up may feel uncomfortable, not because of a lack of confidence, but because of local norms around indirectness or respect. If that's true for you or your team, you can still clarify by using tone softening or group oriented phrasing such as:

- “I may have missed a piece, can I check something?”
- “Would it help if we clarified the next steps together?”

Delivery Tip for All Learners: Clarifying is not just what you say, but how you say it. Your tone, pacing, and body language can either signal confidence and curiosity or accidentally sound abrupt or dismissive.

Pay attention to:

- Tone: Keep it neutral or warm, not too sharp, not overly tentative
- Pacing: Speak slightly slower than normal to allow others to follow (especially across accents or on video)
- Facial expressions: Friendly or interested expressions build trust and psychological safety
- Gestures: Nodding when paraphrasing can show you’re engaged and inviting agreement

For example, saying “Just to clarify…” with raised eyebrows and an even tone makes it feel like a genuine check-in. Saying the same phrase quickly, with a sigh or crossed arms, might feel like a correction, even if that’s not your intent.

** What Makes a Strong Clarifying Response?**

As you practise, aim for a response that includes these elements:

✅ Restates what you understood

✅ Paraphrases (puts it in your own words rather than repeating back)

✅ Ends with a confirming question (e.g., “Is that right?” or “Did I get that?”)

✅ Uses respectful, tone-aware phrasing

❌ Avoids simply saying “got it” or “okay” if things are unclear

❌ Avoids word-for-word repetition that lacks interpretation (this can sound robotic)

Example: “Just to confirm, when you said midweek, did you mean EOD Wednesday or sometime on Thursday?”

This is clear because:

- It paraphrases
- It asks a confirming question
- It shows initiative without assuming or interrupting

Answer Key or Discussion Guide: A good response restates the instruction with slight paraphrasing and ends in a confirming tone:

- “Just to confirm, you’d like the regional numbers to directly match what the client forecasted, right?”
- “So I should double check that our region’s data aligns with the client forecast before finalizing?”

Feedback:

- “Nice, your response shows care and accuracy.”
- “You softened the tone while still being clear, great balance.”
- “If it still feels awkward, try writing or saying it aloud again. Clarity gets easier with practice.”

Key Takeaway: Clarifying doesn’t slow things down, it builds clarity, momentum, and most importantly, trust.

When you paraphrase or ask a confirming question, you're showing that the other person’s message matters enough to get right. That makes people feel seen, heard, and respected. It signals that you're engaged, not just going through the motions.

**Clarification builds trust in several ways:**

- It shows reliability: You’re not making assumptions or guessing.
- It creates psychological safety: Others feel less pressure to "fake it" and more freedom to ask their own questions.
- It demonstrates respect: Rather than interrupting or pushing ahead, you're taking care to align.
- It can reduce power distance: When someone junior clarifies respectfully, or when a leader models clarification humbly, it creates shared accountability.

In fact, paraphrasing can transform how you're perceived. Clarifying isn't just a technical skill, it’s a relational choice. It shows others that you're genuinely interested in what they have to say.

Paraphrasing also validates contributors and helps everyone feel clearer and more included in the conversation, not just those who speak quickly or confidently.

In short: Clarification builds trust because it makes conversations more mutual, more accurate, and more human.

**Before & After: Clarification in Action**

Here’s how asking for clarity sounds, and why it matters.

BEFORE (No Clarifying Question) Team Lead: “Let’s prep the final version of the report for this week.” You: “Will do.”
(But what does “final version” mean? You clean up the draft, thinking it’s ready to send. The project manager expected a formatting overhaul and final sign-off. Misalignment follows.)

You: “Just to check, does that mean clean-up and send? Or do you want a review round first?”

Key Shift: You’ve moved from surface agreement to aligned understanding. You reduce rework, model care, and improve task outcomes.

# <a id="_heading=h.r4kdqkjb6sdd"></a>**8. Glossary**

**Active Clarification** – Asking questions or repeating what you understood to confirm meaning.

**Paraphrasing** – Putting someone’s message into your own words to check you understood it.

**Psychological Safety** – Feeling safe to ask questions, admit mistakes, or say you’re unsure, without fear of looking bad or being judged.

**Confirming Language** – Polite ways to ask if you got it right (e.g., “Did I understand this correctly?”)

**Misalignment** – When people think they agree, but actually don’t.

**Team Alignment** – When everyone clearly understands and agrees on the plan or task.

**Got it** – An informal phrase meaning “I understand.” Often used quickly, but it doesn’t always mean real understanding. Best to confirm.

—--

**Clarify vs. Challenge vs. Correct**

**Clarify:** Asking a question or restating something in your own words to make sure you’ve understood. It’s done with curiosity and care, usually to prevent misunderstanding.
→ Example: “Just to confirm, do you mean the client call is at 10 or noon?”

**Challenge**: Pushing back on an idea, process, or decision, often by offering an alternative or disagreement. While sometimes appropriate, this may involve more risk depending on power dynamics.
→ Example: “I don’t agree with that scope, why are we prioritizing these markets first?”

**Correct: **Pointing out that something said was inaccurate or wrong. This can feel confrontational unless softened with tone and intent.
→ Example: “Actually, that deliverable was already submitted last week.”

Tone & Culture Tip: In direct communication cultures, these three behaviours may all be appropriate in meetings. In indirect or high-context cultures, however, clarification is usually more acceptable than challenge or correction, especially when speaking with a more senior colleague or in a group setting. Learners from indirect communication cultures may feel that even clarification sounds intrusive unless phrased collaboratively (e.g., “Would it help to clarify what we mean by Q3 goals?”).

**Clarify vs. Challenge vs. Correct**

**Clarify:** Asking a question or restating something in your own words to make sure you’ve understood. It’s done with curiosity and care, usually to prevent misunderstanding.
→ Example: “Just to confirm, do you mean the client call is at 10 or noon?”

Tip: You can soften clarification further with framing language:
    “Just to be sure I’m supporting this correctly…”
    “Before I act on that, I’d love to check what you meant by…”
    “Would it help if I restated that in my own words?”

Behavioural Link: Matching your tone to the moment supports
psychological safety, and helps others feel more comfortable asking
for clarity too.
                      Clarify vs. Challenge vs. Correct

Clarify: Asking a question or restating something in your own words to make
sure you’ve understood. It’s done with curiosity and care, usually to prevent
misunderstanding.
   Example: “Just to confirm, do you mean the client call is at 10 or noon?”

Challenge: Pushing back on an idea, process, or decision, often by offering
an alternative or disagreement. While sometimes appropriate, this may
involve more risk depending on power dynamics.
   Example: “I don’t agree with that scope, why are we prioritizing these
   markets first?”

Correct: Pointing out that something said was inaccurate or wrong. This can
feel confrontational unless softened with tone and intent.
   Example: “Actually, that deliverable was already submitted last week.”

Tone & Culture Tip:
   In direct communication cultures, these three behaviours may all be
   appropriate in meetings.
   In indirect or high-context cultures, however, clarification is usually more
   acceptable than challenge or correction, especially when speaking with a
   more senior colleague or in a group setting.
       Learners from indirect communication cultures may feel that even
       clarification sounds intrusive unless phrased collaboratively
       e.g., “Would it help to clarify what we mean by Q3 goals?”
