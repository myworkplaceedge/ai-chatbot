# 1. Lesson Overview

**Lesson Title:** From Listener to Ally

**Pathway: We**

**Skill Focus: **Amplifying others, equitable participation, and respectful advocacy

 360 Content / Synthesia Video 1(is 1, 2, 3) **Workplace Challenge: **Welcome to From listener to ally, where** **Learners move beyond being passive listeners to active allies: people who notice when others are being ignored, create space for equitable input, and use respectful language to advocate for inclusion without putting others on the spot.

**Why ** does this** Matter?: **In any team, it’s not always the best ideas that get heard. It’s often the loudest voices or those with the most perceived authority. Some team members may be overlooked or interrupted, or hesitant to speak if they don’t feel valued and included. At the company level, this can mean that great ideas or opportunities get missed, but on a deeper, human level it means that our colleagues can feel left behind and alone.

**2. Learning Objectives** 360 Content/ Synthesia Video 1 (1, 2, 3)

** By the end of this lesson** learners will be able to:

- Intervene in subtle moments when a colleague’s voice is being overlooked or interrupted.
- Invite participation or amplify contributions from colleagues by using respectful and inclusive language.
- Avoid language that draws unwanted attention or makes others uncomfortable.

# 3. Spark (5–10 minutes)

Picture this: you’re in a brainstorming session with your team. Your colleague Carmen offers a thoughtful idea, and her idea is brushed off. Moments later, someone else makes a similar suggestion, and suddenly it gains traction. Everyone nods. The conversation moves on.

As the meeting goes on, Carmen doesn’t offer any more ideas. Her voice is left behind, and no one seems to have noticed the gap in the conversation.

But you noticed. You saw her disappointment and frustration. And maybe you felt uncomfortable yourself. Maybe you made a note of it. Maybe you thought, *That’s not fair*.

In moments like these, silence can feel like agreement. Taking small actions to support others, especially those whose voices are often overlooked, can shift team dynamics in big ways.

By now, we all know that diverse teams are an incredibly important factor in driving innovation. A 2020 McKinsey report found that companies in the top 25% for racial and ethnic diversity across teams were 36% more likely to outperform their competitors. And according to a Harvard Business Review study, teams with more cognitive diversity were able to make decisions faster by exploring different approaches to problem-solving.

But what good is a diverse team if some of your colleagues are not sharing their ideas, voicing their concerns, and participating in decisions? For your team to be at its most effective, you must share in the task of amplifying the voices that may be quieter around the conference room table.

# 4. Shift (20–25 minutes)

**Synthesia Video: Input: **Article

Your role as an ally to your team mates will require you to observe the behaviors that may be present in your team. It will also require you to learn how to include them while respecting their preferences, cultural approach, and autonomy.

Let’s take a look at some ways you can advocate for your colleagues when they are being minimized.

360 Content** **Article**: Glossary words **

** Passing the Mic**

You might not remember exactly what was said, but you remember the feeling: a pause in the conversation where something important didn’t quite land. Maybe someone made a point that got brushed past.* Maybe a suggestion went unacknowledged. Maybe a teammate tried to speak and got talked over.

These moments often pass quickly. No one raises their voice. Nothing dramatic happens. But something is missing: recognition, engagement, and follow-up.

These small acts of exclusion are called **microaggressions***: subtle actions or comments that communicate exclusion, bias, or disrespect. These moments are often unintentional, but they can have a lasting impact. Microaggressions might show up when someone is repeatedly interrupted, when their ideas are passed over without acknowledgment, or when their contributions are minimized or dismissed. Other times, microaggressions might include inappropriate questions (for example, asking a colleague of South Asian heritage, “No, but where are you *really* from?”) and stereotyping (“We’re going to be just fine for this accounting exercise because Wen-chi is good at math”).

They send quiet signals about who gets heard, who gets credit, and who gets left out.

And while staying silent may feel neutral, in practice, it can reinforce imbalance and discourage participation. So, how can you combat these microaggressions?

**Allyship*** is one way to respond to these dynamics. Being an ally means using your awareness and presence to support a more inclusive environment. It involves creating space for others to speak, redirecting attention when someone is overlooked, and helping ensure that all voices are respected and valued within the team.

**Being an ally means choosing not to stay neutral.**

Being a strong ally to your colleagues isn’t about taking over. In a 2020 study, researchers found that not all team members need to be “heroic voicers [in order] to drive improvements in their organization or team: they can accomplish this by acting as allies to help cultivate others’ valuable ideas so they stay alive and reach implementation” (Satterstrom, Kerrissey, & DiBenigno, 2021).

In other words, contributing powerfully to your team can sometimes mean throwing your **weight*** behind your colleagues’ good ideas. It’s about learning how to gently shift the room’s attention. If you want to truly support those around you, you have to become someone who notices when a voice is being lost, who knows how to help bring it back into the conversation.

** **You can do this in small but powerful ways, ensuring everyone in the room feels seen and valued, not just heard.

This is especially important in multicultural, multilingual teams, where communication norms vary. In some cultures, speaking early and assertively is encouraged. In others, pausing before speaking or waiting to be invited is seen as a sign of thoughtfulness and respect. In fast-moving teams or in virtual meetings, these quieter voices may be missed, not because their contributions aren’t valuable, but because their timing, tone, or phrasing doesn’t match the dominant style.

In those moments, it’s easy to move on. But there’s an opportunity for allyship, intentional leadership, and reinforcement of psychological safety there.

So, what does allyship look like in action? Being a true ally isn’t just as simple as noticing who *isn’t* speaking. It’s also about *how* we invite people into the conversation.

Imagine you’re in a team meeting. Someone shares an idea, and the conversation shifts before anyone responds. You could keep moving. Or, you could pause and say, “Let’s go back to what Tylar was saying a minute ago. I think there’s something valuable there we might have missed.” That simple prompt invites others to re-engage. It also tells your colleague, *I saw you. I valued what you said. You’re not invisible here.*

Allyship can also show up when someone is interrupted. You might say, “Hold on, I’d love to hear Aimee finish her thought.” No confrontation. No blame. Just a small nudge that re-centers someone’s voice and encourages the group to listen fully before responding.

Over time, these kinds of moments build a culture where people feel safer contributing, especially those who have historically been sidelined, under-credited, or uncertain about their place in the conversation.

Sometimes, in our eagerness to be inclusive, we unintentionally cause discomfort. Calling someone out with, “You haven’t said anything yet! What’s going on?” may seem like a prompt for participation, but it often comes across as judgment or pressure. So does saying, “Why don’t you tell everyone what you told me?” And what could be more condescending than “It’s okay, we know English isn’t your first language!”

These moments, though well-meaning, can feel patronizing. They spotlight someone in a way that can feel performative rather than supportive.

Effective amplification avoids this kind of unwanted attention. It creates space without forcing it. A better approach might sound like, “We haven’t heard from Gys yet. Would you like to add anything?” or “I’d be curious to hear your thoughts, if you’d like to share.” The tone is open. The invitation is clear. But the autonomy remains with the individual.

Importantly, allyship is not about being perfect. It’s about being present. You won’t always know what to say in the moment. You might miss a chance. You might stumble. What matters most is your commitment to noticing, to learning, and to showing up again the next time.

You don’t need to be the loudest person in the room to be an ally. You just need to be someone who listens with intention, and then speaks up when it matters.

Because when you do, you’re not just helping someone else be heard. You’re helping your team become more inclusive, more trusting, and ultimately, more effective.

### <a id="_heading=h.w88o8wsc7mv0"></a>360 header What Allyship Sounds Like

As you’ve learned so far, the way you show up as an ally for your colleagues can have a huge impact on their experience as part of the team, and on the team overall! Let’s talk a little bit about how to navigate your position as an ally.

You don’t need to “rescue” people. You don’t need to take over. In fact, the most respectful allyship is subtle. It amplifies without overstepping.

Take a look at some not-so-successful attempts at allyship, and the alternatives that will help your colleague get their voice heard.

360 Content

1. *When someone gets interrupted:*

❌Don’t say:

- “Excuse me, don’t interrupt [Name] while they’re speaking!”
- “You obviously don’t care about what [Name] was saying. How rude!”

These phrases can come across as a bit too aggressive. In your quest for allyship, take care not to accuse or berate anyone on your colleague’s behalf.

✅ Say:

- “Hold on, I’d love to hear the rest of what [Name] was saying.”
- “Let’s come back to [Name]’s point. It was important.”

1. *When someone’s idea is ignored:*

❌Don’t say:

- “Don’t try to take credit for what [Name] just said!”
- “Are you really going to skip over [Name]’s idea?”

It’s important to practice redirection rather than accusation when you’re trying to amplify voices.

✅ Say:

- “That’s actually similar to what [Name] suggested earlier. Maybe we can build on that?”
- “Let’s revisit [Name]’s suggestion. It didn’t get the airtime* it deserved.”

1. *When someone seems hesitant to contribute*

❌Don’t say:

- “The idea you were telling me about yesterday was so good! Why don’t you share it with everyone now?”
- “Don’t be scared, [Name]. Speak up!”

Invite rather than order your colleagues to participate. Don’t volunteer information on their behalf; instead, let them share their thoughts on their own.

✅ Say:

- “We haven’t heard from [Name] yet. Would you like to weigh in?”
	- Take care when naming someone specifically, as calling on someone to participate puts them on the spot*. Use this type of invitation only when you know for sure that someone has something to contribute. Perhaps you’ve heard them begin a sentence that was then interrupted several times, or perhaps they’ve raised a hand to contribute but weren’t called on.
- “For those who have been listening and thinking deeply, now’s a chance. This is a safe place to contribute. All ideas are welcome!”
	- This tactic is better when you aren’t sure that someone actually wants to speak.
- “At this time, I’m inviting everyone present here to share their thoughts and ideas. There are no ‘wrong answers’ or ‘bad ideas.’ I value all of your contributions equally. We’re working together and can reach a better consensus when we hear more opinions, so please share. I’m all ears.”
	- Opening your invitation this widely can signal allyship for those who’ve never experienced it or, more likely, don’t even expect it. In some cultures, voicing one’s opinions is not common practice. If you work with team members who need extra encouragement to participate, this is one way of sending that message clearly.

**Glossary: **360 Content/separate lesson page

**Advocate, verb**: To publicly support or recommend a particular idea, cause, or course of action.
Example: *She ****advocated**** for more flexible work policies to better support employee well-being.*

**To brush past, verb phrase**: To ignore or quickly move over something without giving it proper attention or consideration.
Example:* During the meeting, he ****brushed past**** Teresa’s concerns about resource limitations and focused only on the timeline.*

**Microaggression, noun**: A subtle, often unintentional comment or action that expresses prejudice or discrimination toward a marginalized group.
Example: *Although Sally meant well, Samir experienced her repeated questions about where he was “really from” as ****microaggressions****.*

**Allyship, noun**: The active and ongoing practice of supporting and advocating for people from marginalized or underrepresented groups, especially by those who hold more privilege.
Example: *Effective ****allyship**** involves listening, speaking up against injustice, and using one’s influence to create more inclusive workplaces.

***To throw one's weight behind (something), idiom - verb phrase:** To use influence, power, or authority to support an idea, project, or person.
Example: *The CEO ****threw her weight behind**** the new sustainability initiative, accelerating its approval and funding.*

**Airtime, noun**: The amount of time someone is given to speak or share their views, especially in meetings or public discussions.**
**Example: *In team meetings, it's important to ensure everyone gets equal ****airtime**** to contribute their ideas.*

**To put (someone) on the spot, idiom - verb phrase**: To ask someone a difficult question or force them to make a decision unexpectedly, often in front of others.**
**Example: *She was ****put on the spot**** during the meeting when the CEO asked her to justify the team's budget increase.*

SCENARIO CHARTER
Problem Statement:​
In a team meeting, Sam notices their colleague Yuki's idea being overlooked and later
adopted by someone else without credit. Sam wants to practice allyship but isn't sure how to
intervene without making Yuki uncomfortable or creating conflict.

Learning Objective:​
By the end of this scenario, learners will practice intervening when a colleague's voice is
overlooked, amplifying contributions using respectful language, and avoiding language that
draws unwanted attention.

Success Signal:​
Learner can identify moments requiring allyship, select effective amplification phrases, and
understand the difference between supportive intervention and performative or
uncomfortable spotlight.

Context:​
Sam is part of a diverse marketing team. In today's virtual meeting, Yuki (a quieter,
thoughtful contributor) suggests a creative campaign direction that gets briefly acknowledged
but not discussed. Ten minutes later, Marcus presents a similar idea and the team
enthusiastically engages. Yuki stops contributing. Sam must decide how to act as an ally.

# 1. Lesson Overview

**Lesson Title:** From Listener to Ally

**Pathway: We**

**Skill Focus: **Amplifying others, equitable participation, and respectful advocacy

**Workplace Challenge: **This lesson helps learners move beyond being passive listeners to active allies: people who notice when others are being ignored, create space for equitable input, and use respectful language to advocate for inclusion without putting others on the spot.

**Why It Matters: **In any team, it’s not always the best ideas that get heard. It’s often the loudest voices or those with the most perceived authority. Some team members may be overlooked or interrupted, or hesitant to speak if they don’t feel valued and included. At the company level, this can mean that great ideas or opportunities get missed, but on a deeper, human level it means that our colleagues can feel left behind and alone.

**2. Learning Objectives**

Learners will be able to:

- Intervene in subtle moments when a colleague’s voice is being overlooked or interrupted.
- Invite participation or amplify contributions from colleagues by using respectful and inclusive language.
- Avoid language that draws unwanted attention or makes others uncomfortable.

# 3. Spark (5–10 minutes)

Picture this: you’re in a brainstorming session with your team. Your colleague Carmen offers a thoughtful idea, and her idea is brushed off. Moments later, someone else makes a similar suggestion, and suddenly it gains traction. Everyone nods. The conversation moves on.

But you noticed. You saw her disappointment and frustration. And maybe you felt uncomfortable yourself. Maybe you made a note of it. Maybe you thought, *That’s not fair*.

In moments like these, silence can feel like agreement. Taking small actions to support others, especially those whose voices are often overlooked, can shift team dynamics in big ways.

But what good is a diverse team if some of your colleagues are not sharing their ideas, voicing their concerns, and participating in decisions? For your team to be at its most effective, you must share in the task of amplifying the voices that may be quieter around the conference room table.

# 4. Shift (20–25 minutes)

**Input: **Article

Your role as an ally to your team mates will require you to observe the behaviors that may be present in your team. It will also require you to learn how to include them while respecting their preferences, cultural approach, and autonomy.

Let’s take a look at some ways you can advocate for your colleagues when they are being minimized.

**Passing the Mic**

You might not remember exactly what was said, but you remember the feeling: a pause in the conversation where something important didn’t quite land. Maybe someone made a point that got brushed past.* Maybe a suggestion went unacknowledged. Maybe a teammate tried to speak and got talked over.

These moments often pass quickly. No one raises their voice. Nothing dramatic happens. But something is missing: recognition, engagement, and follow-up.

These small acts of exclusion are called **microaggressions***: subtle actions or comments that communicate exclusion, bias, or disrespect. These moments are often unintentional, but they can have a lasting impact. Microaggressions might show up when someone is repeatedly interrupted, when their ideas are passed over without acknowledgment, or when their contributions are minimized or dismissed. Other times, microaggressions might include inappropriate questions (for example, asking a colleague of South Asian heritage, “No, but where are you *really* from?”) and stereotyping (“We’re going to be just fine for this accounting exercise because Wen-chi is good at math”).

They send quiet signals about who gets heard, who gets credit, and who gets left out.

And while staying silent may feel neutral, in practice, it can reinforce imbalance and discourage participation. So, how can you combat these microaggressions?

**Allyship*** is one way to respond to these dynamics. Being an ally means using your awareness and presence to support a more inclusive environment. It involves creating space for others to speak, redirecting attention when someone is overlooked, and helping ensure that all voices are respected and valued within the team.

**Being an ally means choosing not to stay neutral.**

Being a strong ally to your colleagues isn’t about taking over. In a 2020 study, researchers found that not all team members need to be “heroic voicers [in order] to drive improvements in their organization or team: they can accomplish this by acting as allies to help cultivate others’ valuable ideas so they stay alive and reach implementation” (Satterstrom, Kerrissey, & DiBenigno, 2021).

In other words, contributing powerfully to your team can sometimes mean throwing your weight* behind your colleagues’ good ideas. It’s about learning how to gently shift the room’s attention. If you want to truly support those around you, you have to become someone who notices when a voice is being lost, who knows how to help bring it back into the conversation.

You can do this in small but powerful ways, ensuring everyone in the room feels seen and valued, not just heard.

This is especially important in multicultural, multilingual teams, where communication norms vary. In some cultures, speaking early and assertively is encouraged. In others, pausing before speaking or waiting to be invited is seen as a sign of thoughtfulness and respect. In fast-moving teams or in virtual meetings, these quieter voices may be missed, not because their contributions aren’t valuable, but because their timing, tone, or phrasing doesn’t match the dominant style.

In those moments, it’s easy to move on. But there’s an opportunity for allyship, intentional leadership, and reinforcement of psychological safety there.

So, what does allyship look like in action? Being a true ally isn’t just as simple as noticing who *isn’t* speaking. It’s also about *how* we invite people into the conversation.

Imagine you’re in a team meeting. Someone shares an idea, and the conversation shifts before anyone responds. You could keep moving. Or, you could pause and say, “Let’s go back to what Gys was saying a minute ago. I think there’s something valuable there we might have missed.” That simple prompt invites others to re-engage. It also tells your colleague, *I saw you. I valued what you said. You’re not invisible here.*

Allyship can also show up when someone is interrupted. You might say, “Hold on, I’d love to hear Gys finish his thought.” No confrontation. No blame. Just a small nudge that re-centers someone’s voice and encourages the group to listen fully before responding.

Over time, these kinds of moments build a culture where people feel safer contributing, especially those who have historically been sidelined, under-credited, or uncertain about their place in the conversation.

Sometimes, in our eagerness to be inclusive, we unintentionally cause discomfort. Calling someone out with, “You haven’t said anything yet! What’s going on?” may seem like a prompt for participation, but it often comes across as judgment or pressure. So does saying, “Why don’t you tell everyone what you told me?” And what could be more condescending than “It’s okay, we know English isn’t your first language!”

These moments, though well-meaning, can feel patronizing. They spotlight someone in a way that can feel performative rather than supportive.

Effective amplification avoids this kind of unwanted attention. It creates space without forcing it. A better approach might sound like, “We haven’t heard from Gys yet. Would you like to add anything?” or “I’d be curious to hear your thoughts, if you’d like to share.” The tone is open. The invitation is clear. But the autonomy remains with the individual.

Importantly, allyship is not about being perfect. It’s about being present. You won’t always know what to say in the moment. You might miss a chance. You might stumble. What matters most is your commitment to noticing, to learning, and to showing up again the next time.

You don’t need to be the loudest person in the room to be an ally. You just need to be someone who listens with intention, and then speaks up when it matters.

Because when you do, you’re not just helping someone else be heard. You’re helping your team become more inclusive, more trusting, and ultimately, more effective.

### **What Allyship Sounds Like**

You don’t need to “rescue” people. You don’t need to take over. In fact, the most respectful allyship is subtle. It amplifies without overstepping.

Take a look at some not-so-successful attempts at allyship, and the alternatives that will help your colleague get their voice heard.

1. *When someone gets interrupted:*

❌Don’t say:

- “Excuse me, don’t interrupt [Name] while they’re speaking!”
- “You obviously don’t care about what [Name] was saying. How rude!”

These phrases can come across as a bit too aggressive. In your quest for allyship, take care not to accuse or berate anyone on your colleague’s behalf.

✅ Say:

- “Hold on, I’d love to hear the rest of what [Name] was saying.”
- “Let’s come back to [Name]’s point. It was important.”

1. *When someone’s idea is ignored:*

❌Don’t say:

- “Don’t try to take credit for what [Name] just said!”
- “Are you really going to skip over [Name]’s idea?”

It’s important to practice redirection rather than accusation when you’re trying to amplify voices.

✅ Say:

- “That’s actually similar to what [Name] suggested earlier. Maybe we can build on that?”
- “Let’s revisit [Name]’s suggestion. It didn’t get the airtime* it deserved.”

1. *When someone seems hesitant to contribute*

❌Don’t say:

- “The idea you were telling me about yesterday was so good! Why don’t you share it with everyone now?”
- “Don’t be scared, [Name]. Speak up!”

Invite rather than order your colleagues to participate. Don’t volunteer information on their behalf; instead, let them share their thoughts on their own.

✅ Say:

- “We haven’t heard from [Name] yet. Would you like to weigh in?”
	- Take care when naming someone specifically, as calling on someone to participate puts them on the spot*. Use this type of invitation only when you know for sure that someone has something to contribute. Perhaps you’ve heard them begin a sentence that was then interrupted several times, or perhaps they’ve raised a hand to contribute but weren’t called on.
- “For those who have been listening and thinking deeply, now’s a chance. This is a safe place to contribute. All ideas are welcome!”
	- This tactic is better when you aren’t sure that someone actually wants to speak.
- “At this time, I’m inviting everyone present here to share their thoughts and ideas. There are no ‘wrong answers’ or ‘bad ideas.’ I value all of your contributions equally. We’re working together and can reach a better consensus when we hear more opinions, so please share. I’m all ears.”
	- Opening your invitation this widely can signal allyship for those who’ve never experienced it or, more likely, don’t even expect it. In some cultures, voicing one’s opinions is not common practice. If you work with team members who need extra encouragement to participate, this is one way of sending that message clearly.

Original Script

 ** Passing the Mic**

You might not remember exactly what was said, but you remember the feeling: a pause in the conversation where something important didn’t quite land. Maybe someone made a point that got brushed past.* Maybe a suggestion went unacknowledged. Maybe a teammate tried to speak and got talked over.

These moments often pass quickly. No one raises their voice. Nothing dramatic happens. But something is missing: recognition, engagement, and follow-up.

These small acts of exclusion are called **microaggressions***: subtle actions or comments that communicate exclusion, bias, or disrespect. These moments are often unintentional, but they can have a lasting impact. Microaggressions might show up when someone is repeatedly interrupted, when their ideas are passed over without acknowledgment, or when their contributions are minimized or dismissed. Other times, microaggressions might include inappropriate questions (for example, asking a colleague of South Asian heritage, “No, but where are you *really* from?”) and stereotyping (“We’re going to be just fine for this accounting exercise because Wen-chi is good at math”).

They send quiet signals about who gets heard, who gets credit, and who gets left out.

And while staying silent may feel neutral, in practice, it can reinforce imbalance and discourage participation. So, how can you combat these microaggressions?

**Allyship*** is one way to respond to these dynamics. Being an ally means using your awareness and presence to support a more inclusive environment. It involves creating space for others to speak, redirecting attention when someone is overlooked, and helping ensure that all voices are respected and valued within the team.

**Being an ally means choosing not to stay neutral.**

Being a strong ally to your colleagues isn’t about taking over. In a 2020 study, researchers found that not all team members need to be “heroic voicers [in order] to drive improvements in their organization or team: they can accomplish this by acting as allies to help cultivate others’ valuable ideas so they stay alive and reach implementation” (Satterstrom, Kerrissey, & DiBenigno, 2021).

In other words, contributing powerfully to your team can sometimes mean throwing your **weight*** behind your colleagues’ good ideas. It’s about learning how to gently shift the room’s attention. If you want to truly support those around you, you have to become someone who notices when a voice is being lost, who knows how to help bring it back into the conversation.

** **You can do this in small but powerful ways, ensuring everyone in the room feels seen and valued, not just heard.

This is especially important in multicultural, multilingual teams, where communication norms vary. In some cultures, speaking early and assertively is encouraged. In others, pausing before speaking or waiting to be invited is seen as a sign of thoughtfulness and respect. In fast-moving teams or in virtual meetings, these quieter voices may be missed, not because their contributions aren’t valuable, but because their timing, tone, or phrasing doesn’t match the dominant style.

In those moments, it’s easy to move on. But there’s an opportunity for allyship, intentional leadership, and reinforcement of psychological safety there.

So, what does allyship look like in action? Being a true ally isn’t just as simple as noticing who *isn’t* speaking. It’s also about *how* we invite people into the conversation.

Imagine you’re in a team meeting. Someone shares an idea, and the conversation shifts before anyone responds. You could keep moving. Or, you could pause and say, “Let’s go back to what Tylar was saying a minute ago. I think there’s something valuable there we might have missed.” That simple prompt invites others to re-engage. It also tells your colleague, *I saw you. I valued what you said. You’re not invisible here.*

Allyship can also show up when someone is interrupted. You might say, “Hold on, I’d love to hear Aimee finish her thought.” No confrontation. No blame. Just a small nudge that re-centers someone’s voice and encourages the group to listen fully before responding.

Over time, these kinds of moments build a culture where people feel safer contributing, especially those who have historically been sidelined, under-credited, or uncertain about their place in the conversation.

Sometimes, in our eagerness to be inclusive, we unintentionally cause discomfort. Calling someone out with, “You haven’t said anything yet! What’s going on?” may seem like a prompt for participation, but it often comes across as judgment or pressure. So does saying, “Why don’t you tell everyone what you told me?” And what could be more condescending than “It’s okay, we know English isn’t your first language!”

These moments, though well-meaning, can feel patronizing. They spotlight someone in a way that can feel performative rather than supportive.

Effective amplification avoids this kind of unwanted attention. It creates space without forcing it. A better approach might sound like, “We haven’t heard from Gys yet. Would you like to add anything?” or “I’d be curious to hear your thoughts, if you’d like to share.” The tone is open. The invitation is clear. But the autonomy remains with the individual.

Importantly, allyship is not about being perfect. It’s about being present. You won’t always know what to say in the moment. You might miss a chance. You might stumble. What matters most is your commitment to noticing, to learning, and to showing up again the next time.

You don’t need to be the loudest person in the room to be an ally. You just need to be someone who listens with intention, and then speaks up when it matters.

Because when you do, you’re not just helping someone else be heard. You’re helping your team become more inclusive, more trusting, and ultimately, more effective.
