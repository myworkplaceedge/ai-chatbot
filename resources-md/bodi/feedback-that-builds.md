Feedback That Builds (Title)

Target language phrasing: (sub)

Below are some suggested phrases that can be adopted when giving feedback.

- Here is what went well and what we can improve.
- You did great on [specific], and here is one idea to improve.
- Let’s grow this even better together.

Synthesia Video 4 Explanation: These phrases emphasize collaboration and growth, framing feedback as a shared journey rather than a judgment. Remember to integrate these growth-oriented phrases to soften the delivery and maintain psychological safety.

For example, in the West African culture, a direct approach to providing feedback might be perceived as harsh or disrespectful. Using SBI helps you stay clear without being perceived as harsh.

### Feedback that builds.pdf

"Feedback That Builds" - Branching Scenario

SCENARIO CHARTER
Problem Statement:​
Maya needs to give feedback to her colleague Jordan about a presentation that went poorly,
but she's worried about damaging their working relationship and wants to communicate
respectfully across their different communication styles.

Learning Objective:​
By the end of this scenario, learners will practice structuring feedback using the SBI model
while maintaining psychological safety and using growth-oriented language.

Success Signal:​
Learner can identify effective SBI feedback, recognize how tone/framing affects
relationships, and apply the model in a realistic workplace conversation.

Context:​
Maya (Canadian, indirect communicator) works with Jordan (direct communicator) at a tech
company. Jordan just gave a client presentation that was rushed and missed key metrics.
Maya needs to give feedback.

FULL BRANCHING SCRIPT WITH DECISIONS

SLIDE 1: INTRODUCTION (Situation Setup)

Title: A Feedback Moment

Narration/Text:

"You're Maya, a project coordinator at a tech firm. Your colleague Jordan just finished
presenting quarterly results to an important client. The presentation was rushed—Jordan
spoke very quickly and skipped several key metrics that the client specifically asked about.

The client looked confused and asked to reschedule for more details. Your manager has
asked you to give Jordan some feedback to help them improve before the next presentation.

How will you approach this conversation?"

Navigation: [Continue] → Slide 2
SLIDE 2: DECISION POINT 1 - Opening the Conversation

Title: How will you start the feedback conversation?

Prompt: "You've just caught Jordan in the break room. How do you begin?"

OPTIONS:

A) "Hey Jordan! That presentation went really well overall. You have great energy when you
present. I just wanted to chat about one small thing that might help for next time."

B) "Jordan, can we talk about the client presentation? There were some issues we need to
address."

C) "Jordan, you really need to slow down when you present. You're losing the audience."

Navigation:

   ●​ Option A → Slide 3A
   ●​ Option B → Slide 3B
   ●​ Option C → Slide 3C

SLIDE 3A: Consequence + Feedback (BEST CHOICE)

Dialogue Outcome:

Jordan smiles and sits down. "Sure! I thought it went okay, but I'm open to feedback. What's
up?"

Feedback to Learner:

✅ Correct Choice
Why this works:​
You started with a positive observation ("great energy") before transitioning to feedback. This
builds psychological safety by showing you noticed Jordan's strengths first. Starting with
strengths reduces defensiveness and signals that this is a growth conversation, not criticism.

Reflection Prompt:​
Notice how Jordan's body language stayed open. How did leading with a positive
observation help create safety for this conversation?

Cultural Insight:​
Starting with genuine appreciation is effective across cultures—it shows respect and sets a
collaborative tone rather than a corrective one.

Relationship Note:​
Trust is building. Jordan feels safe and receptive.
Navigation: [Continue] → Slide 4

SLIDE 3B: Consequence + Feedback (PARTIAL)

Dialogue Outcome:

Jordan's smile fades slightly. "Oh… okay. What issues?" Jordan crosses their arms.

Feedback to Learner:

⚠️ Partially Correct
Why this is risky:​
While you're being direct, the word "issues" signals problems without context. This can
trigger defensiveness before you've even explained what happened. You haven't built
psychological safety first.

Reflection Prompt:​
How did Jordan's body language change? What might have caused them to become
guarded?

Cultural Insight:​
Leading with "issues" or "problems" can feel abrupt, especially for people who value
relationship-building in communication. Consider starting with context or a strength first.

Relationship Note:​
Trust is neutral but fragile. Jordan is listening but wary.

Navigation: [Continue] → Slide 4

SLIDE 3C: Consequence + Feedback (POOR CHOICE)

Dialogue Outcome:

Jordan looks frustrated. "What? I thought it went fine. I covered everything I needed to."

Feedback to Learner:

❌ Incorrect Choice
Why this doesn't work:​
You skipped the SBI model completely and jumped straight to vague criticism ("you need to
slow down"). This focuses on Jordan's behavior as a personal flaw rather than explaining the
situation and impact. It also doesn't acknowledge what went well.
Reflection Prompt:​
Why did Jordan react defensively? What was missing from this feedback approach?

Cultural Insight:​
Blunt criticism without context can damage trust, especially when it feels like a personal
attack rather than constructive feedback.

Relationship Note:​
Trust is weakened. Jordan feels attacked and may shut down.

Navigation: [Continue] → Slide 4

SLIDE 4: DECISION POINT 2 - Delivering the SBI Feedback

Title: Now, structure your feedback using the SBI model

Context Text:​
"You've opened the conversation. Now it's time to give the actual feedback. Remember the
SBI model: Situation, Behavior, Impact."

Prompt: "Which feedback statement uses the SBI model effectively?"

OPTIONS:

A) "In this morning's client presentation, you spoke very quickly and skipped over the Q3
revenue metrics that the client specifically asked about. This made it hard for them to follow,
and they asked to reschedule to get those details."

B) "You always rush through presentations. It's becoming a pattern and you really need to
work on your pacing."

C) "The client seemed confused during your presentation. I think you should try to be clearer
next time."

Navigation:

   ●​ Option A → Slide 5A
   ●​ Option B → Slide 5B
   ●​ Option C → Slide 5C

SLIDE 5A: Consequence + Feedback (BEST CHOICE)

Dialogue Outcome:

Jordan nods thoughtfully. "Oh, I didn't realize I skipped those metrics. I was nervous about
the time limit. That makes sense why they wanted to reschedule."
Feedback to Learner:

✅ Correct Choice - Excellent SBI Application
Why this works:​
You applied the full SBI model perfectly:

    ●​ Situation: "In this morning's client presentation"
    ●​ Behavior: "you spoke very quickly and skipped over the Q3 revenue metrics"
    ●​ Impact: "made it hard for them to follow, and they asked to reschedule"

This is specific, objective, and focuses on observable actions—not Jordan's character.
Jordan can clearly understand what happened and why it mattered.

Reflection Prompt:​
How did being specific about the situation and behavior help Jordan understand the
feedback without feeling attacked?

Cultural Insight:​
Using SBI allows you to be clear and direct while still being respectful—it bridges different
communication styles by focusing on facts, not judgment.

Relationship Note:​
Trust is strengthened. Jordan feels respected and understands the issue clearly.

Navigation: [Continue] → Slide 6

SLIDE 5B: Consequence + Feedback (POOR CHOICE)

Dialogue Outcome:

Jordan looks hurt. "That's not fair. I don't always rush. I thought I did fine today."

Feedback to Learner:

❌ Incorrect Choice
Why this doesn't work:​
You used absolute language ("always") and made it about Jordan's character rather than
specific behavior. This skips the SBI model completely and generalizes, which feels like an
attack. There's no clear situation or measurable impact mentioned.

Reflection Prompt:​
What words triggered Jordan's defensive reaction? How could you reframe this to focus on a
specific instance instead of a pattern?
Cultural Insight:​
Generalizations ("always," "never") undermine psychological safety and make feedback feel
personal rather than constructive.

Relationship Note:​
Trust is damaged. Jordan feels judged rather than supported.

Navigation: [Continue] → Slide 6

SLIDE 5C: Consequence + Feedback (PARTIAL)

Dialogue Outcome:

Jordan looks uncertain. "Um, okay. But what specifically should I do differently?"

Feedback to Learner:

⚠️ Partially Correct
Why this needs improvement:​
While you mentioned the impact ("client seemed confused"), you didn't specify the situation
or behavior clearly. "Be clearer" is too vague—Jordan doesn't know what to change. You
missed the opportunity to use SBI to make the feedback actionable.

Reflection Prompt:​
What information is Jordan still missing? How could you make this feedback more specific
using the SBI model?

Cultural Insight:​
Vague feedback may feel polite, but it doesn't help the person improve. Clarity with kindness
is more respectful.

Relationship Note:​
Trust is neutral. Jordan wants to improve but doesn't have clear direction.

Navigation: [Continue] → Slide 6

SLIDE 6: DECISION POINT 3 - Moving Toward Solutions
(Growth-Oriented Language)

Title: Help Jordan move forward

Context Text:​
"You've given the feedback using SBI. Now, how will you frame the path forward to maintain
psychological safety and support Jordan's growth?"
Prompt: "What do you say next?"

OPTIONS:

A) "I know you care about delivering great presentations. Let's work together on a strategy
for next time—maybe creating a checklist of key points to cover so you don't miss anything
under time pressure."

B) "Just try to remember to include all the metrics next time. It's really important."

C) "This keeps happening, and it's affecting our client relationships. You need to fix this."

Navigation:

   ●​ Option A → Slide 7A
   ●​ Option B → Slide 7B
   ●​ Option C → Slide 7C

SLIDE 7A: Consequence + Feedback (BEST CHOICE)

Dialogue Outcome:

Jordan's face brightens. "That's a great idea! A checklist would really help me stay on track
when I'm nervous. Can we work on one together?"

Feedback to Learner:

✅ Correct Choice - Growth-Oriented Language
Why this works:​
You used high-impact, growth-oriented language:

   ●​ Acknowledged Jordan's positive intent ("I know you care")
   ●​ Framed feedback as collaboration ("Let's work together")
   ●​ Offered a specific, actionable solution (checklist)

This maintains psychological safety while moving toward improvement. Jordan feels
supported, not blamed.

Reflection Prompt:​
Which specific phrase helped Jordan feel like you're on their team rather than criticizing
them?

Cultural Insight:​
Collaborative language ("let's") builds trust across all cultures and shifts feedback from
judgment to partnership.
Relationship Note:​
Trust is fully restored and strengthened. Jordan feels motivated and supported.

Navigation: [Continue] → Slide 8 (Conclusion)

SLIDE 7B: Consequence + Feedback (PARTIAL)

Dialogue Outcome:

Jordan nods slowly. "Yeah, okay. I'll try to remember."

Feedback to Learner:

⚠️ Partially Correct
Why this could be better:​
While polite, this statement is too vague and puts all the responsibility on Jordan to
"remember" without offering support or a concrete strategy. You missed the opportunity to
collaborate and provide actionable next steps.

Reflection Prompt:​
How might Jordan be feeling right now? What could you offer to make this feedback more
actionable and supportive?

Cultural Insight:​
Minimizing the issue ("just try") can feel dismissive and doesn't show investment in Jordan's
growth.

Relationship Note:​
Trust is neutral. Jordan will comply but may not feel truly supported.

Navigation: [Continue] → Slide 8 (Conclusion)

SLIDE 7C: Consequence + Feedback (POOR CHOICE)

Dialogue Outcome:

Jordan's jaw tightens. "I said I didn't realize. I'll handle it." Jordan gets up and leaves
abruptly.

Feedback to Learner:

❌ Incorrect Choice
Why this doesn't work:​
You doubled down on criticism ("this keeps happening") without offering solutions or support.
This destroys psychological safety and makes Jordan feel blamed rather than helped. The
relationship is now strained.

Reflection Prompt:​
How did this response shut down the conversation? What impact will this have on your
working relationship with Jordan?

Cultural Insight:​
Harsh, repetitive criticism damages trust and discourages future openness—especially
without offering help or collaboration.

Relationship Note:​
Trust is broken. Jordan feels attacked and will likely avoid future feedback conversations
with you.

Navigation: [Continue] → Slide 8 (Conclusion)

SLIDE 8: CONCLUSION & LEARNER TAKEAWAY

Title: What Did You Learn?

Content:

The SBI Model in Action:

You just practiced giving feedback using the Situation-Behavior-Impact model. Here's what
makes feedback effective:

1. Social-Emotional Competency​
Recognize emotions (yours and theirs) and create psychological safety before delivering
feedback.

2. Cultural Fluency​
Adapt your communication style to be clear AND respectful, bridging different preferences
for directness.

3. Language for Impact​
Use growth-oriented phrases like:

   ●​ "Here's what went well and what we can improve"
   ●​ "Let's work together on..."
   ●​ "I know you care about..."

4. Relationship Building​
Feedback delivered with respect and collaboration strengthens trust—feedback delivered
with judgment damages it.

Reflection Questions:
   ●​ How did your choices affect Jordan's willingness to receive feedback?
   ●​ Which phrases built psychological safety, and which undermined it?
   ●​ How might you adapt your approach when giving feedback to someone with a
      different communication style?

Next Steps:​
Use the Feedback Prep Worksheet to plan a real feedback conversation this week. Choose
a peer you trust and practice the SBI model.

Facilitator Manual – Feedback That Builds

# Before the Session

Prep Time: 10–15 minutes

• Review the lesson plan, shift content video (A Framework for Giving Impactful Feedback) and feedback prep worksheet.
• Test audio and video for the shift content video.
• Prepare a slide or visual showing the session’s learning objectives.

# Section 1 – Welcome & Objectives (5 minutes)

Say:
“Hi everyone! Welcome to Workplace Edge. Today’s session is called Feedback That Builds. We’ll explore how to give feedback that is constructive, respectful, and focused on growth—especially important in diverse teams where communication styles may vary. You’ll learn a simple model that helps you stay clear and kind when offering feedback.”

Then display on screen and read aloud:

- Structure feedback using the** SBI (Situation-Behaviour-Impact)** model
- Practice empathetic listening and foster psychological safety in conversations
- Use high-impact language to support a growth-oriented team culture

# Section 2 – Spark: Emotional Reflection (5–10 minutes)

Say:
“Let’s begin by thinking about feedback you've received in the past. Maybe from a manager, teammate, or even a peer. Think about the impact it had.”

**Instructions:**

- Read aloud the Spark questions:
	- What made the feedback helpful or unhelpful?
	- How would you describe the feedback – Positive, Negative or Constructive
	- How did it affect your relationship with the person giving it?
	- Did it motivate or discourage you?

• Allow 2-3 minutes for silent reflection or journaling
• Invite 1–2 participants to share (optional)

**Transition Line:**

 “Feedback isn’t just about fixing things. When done well, it builds trust and shows respect—especially across cultures and communication styles.”

# Section 3 – Shift: Learning the SBI Model (20–25 minutes)

Say:
“Now let’s explore a tool called the SBI Model—used by professionals worldwide to give feedback that is specific and respectful.”

Instructions:
• Play the “A Framework for Giving Impactful Feedback” video
• Distribute transcript (if online or accessibility is needed)

Ask learners to note:

- What SBI stands for
- How it helps make feedback clear

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Say:
“Let’s practice using the SBI model in a workplace scenario.”

**Instructions**

Read aloud “Imagine a colleague gave a presentation but spoke very fast and skipped important details. You want to help them improve.”

Ask learners to draft an SBI statement:

- Situation – When/where it happened
- Behavior – What was observed
- Impact – How it affected the team/work

Share the model answer if needed: “In this morning’s team meeting, you presented the project updates very quickly and skipped over key metrics, which made it hard for us to follow and prepare for the next steps.”

## Knowledge Check (5 minutes)

Say:

“Let’s check your understanding. Which of these is the best SBI feedback example?

Instructions:

- Read the 3 options aloud or display them.
- Ask learners to vote or choose wisely.
- Discuss the correct answer: **Option C**

# Section 4 – Stretch: Real World Application (20 minutes)

Say:
“Now it’s your turn. Over the next week, you will try giving real feedback using the SBI model.”

Instructions

- Share the **Feedback Prep Worksheet**
- Review the tone check and phrasing tips
- Ask learners to identify one real opportunity this week to give feedback using the SBI model
- Remind them to choose a peer they feel safe practicing with
- Encourage them to draft their message using the worksheet

# Post-Session Follow-Up (5–7 days later)

Instructions:

- Ask learners to apply the SBI model in real life
- Responses can be written or shared as a comment within the Workplace Edge community.
