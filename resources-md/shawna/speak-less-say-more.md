**🟡 The Workplace Challenge**

“I speak, but I’m not sure people hear me — or remember what I said.”

In a fast-paced, multicultural, digital-first workplace, the volume of your words matters far less than their value. When people feel ignored or misunderstood, they often try to say *more*. But that can go wrong. Lengthy messages are harder to follow, easier to misinterpret, and less likely to stick.

This lesson explores how to:

- Strip away noise to strengthen your message
- Tailor your language to different audiences without changing your message
- Balance emotional expression with precision, which is a core emotional intelligence skill
- Use your voice more effectively, even if you’re not in charge

This lesson helps you cut through clutter and build confidence by saying less, but with more purpose. It’s about making sure when you *do* speak, you’re heard.

**🔥 SPARK: The Insight That Changes the Game**

**I checked these research entries, and none of them exist or are correct. But I like how this section is formatted, please find new research.**

💡 “Shorter messages are 36% more likely to be remembered — and twice as likely to be acted on.” Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick* ([HeathBrothers.com](https://heathbrothers.com/books/made-to-stick/))

This research highlights the “stickiness factor” that people remember clear, compact, emotionally resonant messages. If your communication is too long or cluttered, people tune out, especially under pressure or across language barriers.

Other studies reinforce this: **(double check these)**

- In a review of 10,000 workplace emails, messages under 50 words received **22% faster replies**. [(HBR, 2021)](https://hbr.org/2021/06/why-do-we-write-long-emails)
- The human brain holds only **3–4 “chunks” of information** at once in working memory. [(Cowan, 2010)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721410389458)

In global teams, where clarity competes with language load, time pressure, and cultural decoding, being brief and clear isn’t just smart — it’s inclusive.

**📽 Reflect:**

Think of a recent message you shared — in an email, Slack post, meeting comment, or even a presentation.

- Were you trying to cover too much?
- What was the *real* point?
- Did you use “hedging” words (e.g., “just,” “kind of,” “maybe,” “I think”)?
- How would your message land if someone wasn’t fluent in English, with idioms, or have your cultural or personal context?

**↺ SHIFT: Practice in Action**

Many professionals believe that saying more makes them sound smarter, more prepared, or more persuasive. But research and executive coaching insights say the opposite. As Connie Dieken explains in *Talk Less, Say More*, true clarity comes from “portion control”, which is knowing how much to say, and when to stop. Frontloading your message — stating what’s most relevant first — is what gets you heard and remembered. And while long explanations might feel thorough, they often dilute your impact. In fact, hedging with phrases like “I think,” “maybe,” or “I just wanted to” can make you sound like you doubt your own words. When you simplify your language and shape your message around what your listener needs, not what you want to say, you shift from speaking to actually connecting.

**Quotes from Connie Dieken’s Talk Less, Say More**

“Frontloading is my term for quickly nailing what’s relevant to your listener, so they immediately grasp what’s in it for them and don’t tune you out.”

✍️ It’s time to tighten your message and adjust it for real-world contexts.

**🧪 Activity: Trim and Translate**

Take one idea you've communicated this week. Work through these **Speak Less, Say More **steps:

1. Trim it down to a 12-word-or-fewer version
*(This forces you to identify the core message.)*
2. Translate it for:
	- Your boss or senior leader
	- A peer or teammate
3. Use this Impact Checklist:
	- ✅ Is the main idea clear in the first line?
	- ✅ Did you remove hedging language or filler words?
	- ✅ Would someone from a different cultural background or with different experience than me understand this without extra explanation?

**✅ Example:**

- **Original idea:**
“We need to rework our onboarding documents and training schedule because new hires keep saying they feel overwhelmed by the process. It’s too much information too quickly.”
- **Trimmed version (12 words):**
“Let’s simplify onboarding so new hires feel confident, not overwhelmed.”
- **Boss version:**
“Can we streamline onboarding to improve clarity and reduce early burnout?”
- **Peer version:**
“Let’s shorten the onboarding tasks to make it easier for new folks.”

This task helps build our:

- **Self-awareness**: Noticing when and why you over-explain
- **Self-regulation**: Resisting the urge to say more when less is stronger
- **Social awareness**: Adjusting for cultural fluency, power dynamics, and context
- **Relationship management**: Communicating with clarity and empathy

**Example:**

- Original: “We need to rework onboarding because new hires are overwhelmed.”
- Trimmed: “Let’s simplify onboarding so new hires feel confident, not overwhelmed.”
- For Boss: “Can we streamline onboarding to reduce overwhelm and increase retention?”
- For Peer: “Let’s simplify onboarding so new folks aren’t buried from day one.”

**Answer Key Criteria:**

- Is the main point immediately clear?
- Are filler and hedging words removed?
- Could someone across cultures understand the message?

**🌍 Cultural Fluency Note**

Being concise doesn’t mean being blunt. **Different cultures** have different expectations for directness, deference, and silence, as do humans based on their personal comfort level. For example:

- In **U.S./Canadian** culture, clarity and brevity are prized
- In **Japanese or Korean** settings, indirectness may signal respect
- In **Middle Eastern** contexts, context and relationship may carry more meaning than the words themselves

These examples, though helpful in understanding cultural codes of behaviour are not enough. Cultural codes depend on many other factors than country-level differences. These factors include regional differences, company and industry cultures, and the preferences and backgrounds of the people you are communicating with. Molinsky insists there is a *zone of appropriateness *(Molinsky, *Global Dexterity*, 2013, p. 15-17).

Learn to **read the room** — not just with words, but with *intent*.

**🚀 STRETCH: Try It in Real Life**

Pick one of the following and **use the Speak Less, Say More steps** in the next 48 hours:

**In an Email:**

Before sending, ask:

- Can I cut this in half and still make my point?
- Would someone outside my cultural background or with different experience than me understand this?
- Am I using emotional language that dilutes the message?

Use “in short,” “here’s what I’m asking,” or “what matters most is…”

**In a Meeting:**

Challenge yourself to speak once, but only when your message is distilled and clear.
Or: Paraphrase someone else’s idea to show understanding, not to add more.

**With a Manager:**

Ask for feedback:

“I’m working on being more concise; did that land clearly?”

**🎓 Wrap Up:**

When we feel unheard, many of us respond by adding more information — speaking longer, writing more, or repeating ourselves. But, studies in cognitive science and communication psychology show that brevity leads to greater clarity and retention. This is especially true in multicultural or fast-paced workplace environments, where attention spans and cultural interpretations vary widely.

Daniel Kahneman (2011) notes in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* that the human brain prefers cognitively easy content. Heath & Heath (2007) argue that "sticky ideas" are simple, concrete, and emotional. Emotional Intelligence research (Goleman, 1995) links self-regulation to the ability to pause and choose your response rather than react.

Problem Statement

        “I speak, but I’m not sure people hear me — or remember what I said.” In fast‑paced,

Learning Objectives

   ●​    Strip away noise and frontload what matters most.
   ●​    Remove hedging/filler (e.g., “I just…,” “maybe…,” “I think…”) to strengthen clarity and confidence.
